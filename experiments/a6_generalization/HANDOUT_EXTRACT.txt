The DNA dataset.
We study next-token prediction on biological sequences from the Arc Institute’s OpenGenome2, the
corpus used to train Evo 2. Our course subset mixes metagenomes, eukaryotic genic windows, bacterial
and archaeal genomes, mRNA, viral sequences, noncoding RNA, organelles, and promoters. We take
bounded prefixes of raw FASTA sources at a pinned revision, with quotas approximating the published
raw-base mixture.
Preparation and vocabulary. We discard FASTA headers, uppercase sequences, and tokenize each char-
acter separately. The 19-token vocabulary contains A/C/G/T, eleven ambiguity symbols, U and X, and
only two special tokens: BOS and EOS. We preserve ambiguous characters, append EOS to each record,
and concatenate records within each source without padding. Each 1,023-token payload gets a BOS pre-
fix, making a 1,024-token chunk that may cross record boundaries. Predict the next token causally; BOS
is context only. No species tags or reverse-complement augmentation are added.
The assignment pool. After a fixed random chunk shuffle, we select 30,000 training chunks: 30.72M
tokens including BOS/EOS, plus a disjoint 1,000-chunk validation set. Use this same validation set for
hyperparameter selection and all reported NLL comparisons. Problems 1–3 reuse this pool; Problem 4
varies fine-tuning data. Chunks are disjoint, but neighboring genomic regions or homologous sequences
may cross splits: this tests generalization within the mixture, not to held-out species or genomes. Use the
supplied prepared splits.

Generalization motivation.
This assignment is a major departure from the typical pre-training setup that we have. Instead of limiting ourselves
to one pass of a 600M dataset with d8 architecture, we will instead consider a 30M dataset that we can train on as
many times as we like. The goal is to get the best possible held-out loss using these 30M tokens.
To get more value out of our limited data, the two simplest methods are (1) epoching (2) scaling parameter count.
Following the investigations in Kim et al. (2025), we can establish a baseline where we tune learning rate, weight
decay, and epoch count for each model size and method. We find that despite noise, there is a trend where larger
models end up getting better loss. However, this trend plateaus, meaning that we can’t simply scale model size for
arbitrarily lower losses. Furthermore, this improvement on validation loss translates to downstream benchmarks
(specifically, the DART task (Patel et al., 2024) where we measure whether a model can distinguish between a real
sequence and a randomly shuffled variant of the same sequence)
In this assignment, you will assess different ways of reducing the loss in this regime where we are limited by data.
To scope our investigation, we will mostly be training d8’s. A good starting point is LR 0.003, WD 3.2, and 32
epochs.

Problem 4: Fine-tuning
One of the most effective ways to improve data efficiency is to first train on related data, then fine-
tune on the target dataset. Hernandez, Kaplan et al. (2021), Scaling Laws for Transfer, study this
benefit in terms of the effective amount of data transferred by pretraining. Here we compare four
starting points:
Related DNA: pretraining on eukaryotic genic windows from OpenGenome2, excluding the target
and validation chunks; DCLM text: pretraining on natural-language text; nucleotide noise: pre-
training on IID uniform A/C/G/T, with BOS at the start of each sequence; and scratch: random
initialization.
We release the three pretrained checkpoints with the assignment; the original pretraining datasets
are not supplied. Each checkpoint was trained from scratch with the same d8 transformer body
and next-token objective for approximately 10B tokens in 1,024-token sequences. Related-DNA
pretraining repeats its approximately 2.57M eligible chunks about 3.8 times. DCLM uses a text
tokenizer, so we transfer its transformer body and randomly initialize the DNA embedding and
output head; the two nucleotide checkpoints retain their DNA embedding and head. Pretraining is
already complete; your experiments start from these checkpoints or from scratch. Fine-tuning runs
are not supplied. In (a) and (b), use full fine-tuning, updating all parameters.
(a) Full fine-tuning. On the 30k-sequence target pool, compare all four initializations after 1, 4,
and 16 passes over the fine-tuning data. Tune the fine-tuning LR for each initialization and pass
count. Which starting point achieves the lowest validation loss, and how does its advantage
change with additional passes?
(b) Scaling and effective samples. Use fine-tuning pools of n ∈ {1k, 3k, 10k, 30k} sequences, train-
ing for 16 passes over each pool. Build a scaling law for validation loss as a function of fine-
tuning sample count, with the four initializations on one plot. How do their scaling laws com-
pare? Can you map the benefit of pretraining to an effective sample count on the target task?
(c) Adapting related DNA. Focus on the related-DNA initialization. Investigate tuning weight
decay, regularizing weights toward their pretrained initialization instead of zero, freezing only
the output head, and freezing the transformer body (updating only the embedding and output
head). Compare these variants with your full-fine-tuning baseline. Which adaptations help?
(d) Exploration. Can strategies from Problems 1–3 or previous assignments also improve data
efficiency when fine-tuning? Investigate a strategy of your choice and compare it with your
full-fine-tuning baseline.
